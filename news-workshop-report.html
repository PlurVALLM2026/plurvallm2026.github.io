Workshop report · 19 November 2026

Researchers examine whose values AI should reflect

The first PlurVA-LLM workshop combined four keynotes with research on cultural values, model training and evaluation, alongside a shared task spanning China, Indonesia and Sri Lanka.

Researchers examined how artificial intelligence should respond to cultural differences and conflicting values at the first PlurVA-LLM workshop in Hengqin, Zhuhai, China, on November 10, 2026.

The First Workshop on Pluralistic Value Alignment of Large Language Models, held as part of AACL-IJCNLP 2026, brought together researchers in natural language processing, AI safety, philosophy and the social sciences. Participants joined in person and online.

Submissions and shared-task entries came from countries including China, the US, the UK, Germany, India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Turkey, Pakistan, Ghana, Brazil, Mexico and Luxembourg.

The organising team included researchers from Tianjin University, the University of Lisbon, Macau University of Science and Technology, the University of California, Riverside, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, and the Singapore University of Technology and Design. Students from Tianjin University and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, helped with local arrangements.

The day featured four keynotes, three oral sessions, a poster session and the Best Paper and Best System awards. Eleven studies were presented in the oral sessions, and the poster programme comprised 16 works. Topics ranged from theories of value alignment to cross-cultural evaluation, training methods and social applications.

The workshop proceedings are available in the ACL Anthology.

Submissions and participation

The workshop received 38 submissions: 20 long papers, 13 short papers and five shared-task papers. Twenty-one were accepted, an acceptance rate of 55.3 per cent.

Nine of the 20 submissions to the archival track were accepted, as were both submissions through ACL Rolling Review and five of the 11 non-archival submissions. The five shared-task papers comprised four system reports and an overview by the organisers.

A further 12 papers already accepted at other conferences were submitted for presentation. The programme scheduled 28 papers, with 12 oral presentations and 16 posters, involving 24 presenters. Eleven of the scheduled oral presentations took place on the day.

The submissions covered multilingual and low-resource language modelling, cultural and pragmatic aspects of value understanding, and evaluation in education. They included theoretical studies, prototype systems and shared-task benchmarks.

The accompanying word cloud highlights recurring themes in the papers, including culture, benchmarking, evaluation, low-resource languages, datasets and human values. Methods ranged from preference learning and reinforcement learning to fine-tuning and safety analysis, with applications including social robots.

A shared task across three countries

The shared task asked teams to build a single model system that could make judgements consistent with human annotations in three country-specific datasets: D2VBench from China, Pancasila-Dilemmas from Indonesia and LKValues from Sri Lanka.

It offered a resource-constrained track and an open track. Final rankings used the macro-average of accuracy across the three datasets, giving equal weight to each country’s dataset.

Twenty teams registered from China, Sri Lanka, the US, Indonesia, India and Bangladesh. Four teams ultimately submitted five valid track entries, representing Chinese universities, Sri Lankan research institutions and independent researchers.

All four finalist teams exceeded their track’s baseline on the overall score, using methods including LoRA fine-tuning and country-specific prompts.

The commercial-model experiments produced modest gains over the open-track baseline. Among the finalist systems, accuracy was lowest on the Indonesian dilemma dataset. Differences in dataset design and annotation limit direct comparisons between countries.

The datasets have been released publicly to support further work on cross-cultural value alignment.

The opening

Deyi Xiong, a professor at Tianjin University, opened the workshop by asking whose values language models should reflect and how they should respond when people reasonably disagree.

He welcomed participants joining in person and online, thanked the speakers, authors, reviewers, shared-task teams, organisers and volunteers, and acknowledged the support of DataOcean AI and Chaoxing.

The questions set the agenda for discussions linking technical approaches to alignment with perspectives from culture, philosophy and law.

Four perspectives on alignment

Human intelligence and AI

Rostam J. Neuwirth, distinguished professor of law and head of the Department of Global Legal Studies at the University of Macau, delivered the first keynote, “Pluralism and the Paradox of AI Alignment: Rethinking Human Intelligence in the Age of AI”. His research connects law, language, cognition, culture and technology, including questions of AI governance.

Neuwirth argued that alignment works in both directions. AI systems must adapt to human values, while people need to develop the cognitive capacities to work effectively with them. As AI systems learn, adapt and make decisions, he asked how human intelligence itself should develop. His talk extended the discussion from model behaviour to the ways people respond to advances in AI.

Safety across cultures

Roy Ka-Wei Lee, an associate professor of computer science at the University of British Columbia, gave the second keynote, “Whose Values, Whose Safety? Building LLM Safety for a Pluralistic World”. His work spans responsible AI, social computing, natural language processing and online safety, including hate speech and safety in low-resource languages.

Lee examined how understandings of safety and harm differ across cultures, languages and social groups. As language models shape communication and access to information, he argued, safety evaluation must account for those differences.

Drawing on work on group values, distributed evaluation and online harm detection, he considered how models could represent legitimate differences, how safety could be assessed where there was no shared norm, and how underrepresented groups could be included in that assessment.

AI in its social setting

Nancy F. Chen, a principal investigator at A*STAR’s Center for Frontier AI Research in Singapore, delivered “Grounding AI in Society”. She leads the centre’s research on ethical and trustworthy AI, with interests spanning language, speech, multimodal models and human–AI collaboration. She is a fellow of ISCA and AAIA and was a programme chair for NeurIPS 2025 and ICLR 2023.

Chen examined what AI needs to understand about the social settings in which it operates. Beyond processing language and other inputs, systems must account for relationships, cultural differences and the circumstances in which people make decisions. Her talk considered how AI could support people’s autonomy and capabilities, and how collaboration between people and AI could work in everyday life.

The limits of pluralistic alignment

Pak-Hang Wong, an assistant professor at Hong Kong Baptist University and a research fellow at its Centre for Applied Ethics, delivered the final keynote, “Pluralistic Alignment Always, Sometimes, or Never? Taking Value Pluralism Seriously”. His research examines the social, ethical and political implications of technology from cross-cultural perspectives. He co-edited Harmonious Technology: A Confucian Ethics of Technology.

Wong asked under what conditions differences in values warrant a pluralistic response from AI. He distinguished differences in preference from reasonable disagreements that deserve to be preserved. Some disagreements, he noted, arise from error, ignorance or bias.

His talk considered how to judge whether disagreement is reasonable and under what conditions it should shape a model’s responses. He questioned the assumption that every subjective or value-laden question calls for a pluralistic answer.

Across the four talks, alignment emerged as a question about the relationship between people and AI, the meaning of safety in different communities, and the grounds for preserving disagreement.

Research presentations

The three oral sessions featured 11 studies of value evaluation, training, social reasoning, cross-cultural understanding and shared-task systems.

First oral session

Junru Wu, of Tianjin University in China, presented “EduValueBench: Benchmarking Situated Value Alignment of LLMs in Chinese Educational Decision-Making”. The expert-led benchmark examines how closely models’ answers about educational decisions match experts’ value judgements.

Aayush Singh, an independent researcher, investigated the effect of training-data structure in “Denser, Not Wider: Training-Data Structure Selects the Kind of Pluralism a Fine-Tuned Model Retains”. The controlled experiments compared debate-style training with separate accounts of different positions, measuring the range of views within one answer and across repeated answers.

Alex Chandy, of the University of Maryland, College Park, in the US, examined the effects of supervised fine-tuning and direct preference optimisation in “Effects of Post-Training on Cultural Anchoring in LLMs”. The study introduced the concept of cultural anchoring and found persistent cultural biases in post-trained models.

“Guided Relational Analysis Improves Social Intelligence in LLMs”, presented by Anvesh Rao Vijjini, of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and Oracle in the US, examined whether explicit analysis of interpersonal relationships could improve models’ social reasoning. The method helped models recognise psychological manipulation and breaches of social norms.

Second oral session

Suraj Ranganath, of the University of California, San Diego, in the US, presented “StealthRL: Reinforcement Learning Paraphrase Attacks for Multi-Detector Evasion of AI-Text Detectors”. The study used reinforcement learning to adapt paraphrasing attacks and found that attacks could transfer across several detectors, exposing weaknesses in their ability to identify AI-generated text.

Tarun Chinta, an independent researcher, examined what happens when the usefulness of a model’s output changes as people adopt it. The paper, “Performative Alignment: Preference Learning under Deployment-Dependent Utility”, analysed the limits of fixed reward models in these settings.

Ziyan Yang, of Jinan University in China, presented “Country Name or Cultural Evidence? A Causal Audit of Population-Grounded Steering in Large Language Models”. The counterfactual audit found that explicit evidence about the distribution of opinions within a population generally had more influence on model responses than country names, including when the two conflicted.

Jinu Nyachhyon, of the Institute for Research and Innovation in Intelligent Systems in Nepal, presented “NepPlural: A Human-Anchored Benchmark for Persona Annotation in Nepali Migration Discourse”. The benchmark tests how reliably models assign sociocultural personas to discussions of youth emigration and brain drain, and how annotation procedures and a “no persona” option affect their decisions.

Third oral session

Supryadi, of Tianjin University in China, presented “Findings of the PlurVA-LLM 2026 Shared Task on Pluralistic Value Alignment Across China, Indonesia, and Sri Lanka”. The report set out the task’s three national contexts and compared the performance of fine-tuning and prompt engineering in cross-cultural value alignment.

Nevidu Jayatilleke, of the University of Moratuwa in Sri Lanka, presented “PlurVA-LLM-2026 Shared Task Track-1: Pluralistic Value Alignment in LLMs via Multilingual Fine-Tuning and Threshold Calibration”. The system combined multilingual fine-tuning, targeted data augmentation and conditional threshold calibration to address cross-cultural alignment under resource constraints.

Ghazal Kalhor, of EPFL in Switzerland, presented “MasalBench: A Benchmark for Contextual and Cross-Cultural Understanding of Persian Proverbs in LLMs”. The study found a gap between models’ ability to recognise Persian proverbs in context and their ability to match them to equivalent English proverbs.

Discussion between sessions

Discussions continued during the coffee breaks, as participants compared research methods, raised questions from the talks and explored possible collaborations across disciplines and cultural contexts.

Awards for research and system design

The Best Paper Award went to Ziyan Yang and Kuo Zhao for “Country Name or Cultural Evidence? A Causal Audit of Population-Grounded Steering in Large Language Models”.

The study used four controlled experimental conditions to separate the effects of country labels from those of population-level survey evidence. Across four models, explicit survey evidence generally influenced responses more strongly than country labels, particularly when the two conflicted. The award recognised the paper’s clear research question, controlled design and consistent findings across the models tested.

Joydeb Mondal received the Best System Award for “PlurVA-LLM-2026 Shared Task Track-1 and 2: Preference Learning for Pluralistic Value Alignment”. His system combined candidate-answer classification with pairwise preference ranking to learn from the datasets’ different annotation formats.

The system ranked first in both tracks, with a macro-average accuracy of 80.8 per cent across the three datasets, using a model with fewer than eight billion parameters. The paper also examined how annotation formats and evaluation targets affected performance across the country-specific datasets.

Poster presentations

The poster programme featured 16 studies on cultural and regional bias, value concepts, training data, evaluation and personalised applications. Some presenters gave brief introductions before the session, which offered time to discuss findings, methods and limitations. The following studies were among those featured.

Akash Anil, of the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Bhopal (IISER Bhopal) in India, presented “IndRegBias: A Dataset for Studying Indian Regional Biases in English and Code-Mixed Social Media Comments”. Its 25,000 comments support tests of whether models can detect regional bias and assess its severity in English and mixed-language text.

Mark J. Hill, of King’s College London in the UK, examined how models understand and apply two conceptions of liberty in “Concepts at Work: Testing Rival Conceptions of Liberty in Large Language Models”. The study compared non-interference with non-domination and considered how these concepts shape practical judgements.

Oleg Smirnov, of Microsoft Gaming in Stockholm, Sweden, presented “The Language You Ask In: Language-Conditioned Ideological Divergence in LLM Analysis of Contested Political Documents”. Comparing two models’ interpretations of the same contested document, the study found that Russian and Ukrainian prompts elicited different interpretive stances in this case.

Yuemei Xu, of the School of Information Science and Technology at Beijing Foreign Studies University in China, presented “C-VoicesCompass: Evaluating Value Alignment of LLMs with Chinese Social Values”. The dataset and lightweight evaluation framework cover 12 dimensions of Chinese social values and were used to compare eight LLMs.

Philipp E. Glass, of Brunel University London in the UK, presented “CompOrca: Corpus-Scale Compliance Labelling of Instruction-Tuning Data”. The work labelled more than 4.23 million OpenOrca examples for instruction compliance, with checks against a human-reviewed sample, providing a resource for studying refusals and unfulfilled requests in fine-tuning data.

Junyu Lu, of Dalian University of Technology in China, presented “PlurVA-LLM-2026 Shared Task Track-2: PluriGuide—Principle Distillation and Identifier-Bound Demonstrations for Pluralistic Value Alignment”. The method distilled culture-specific decision principles from annotated examples and linked them to a small set of demonstrations used in prompts to guide cross-cultural value judgements.

Nevidu Jayatilleke, of the University of Moratuwa in Sri Lanka, also presented “PlurVA-LLM-2026 Shared Task Track-1: Curriculum Fine-Tuning with Soft Labels and Positional Consistency for Cross-Cultural Value Alignment”. This parameter-efficient approach combined soft labels, consistency across answer-option positions and a staged multilingual training curriculum.

Nyx Redondo, of the Ronin Institute in the US and the IamI.Earth Foundation in Sweden, presented “The Alignment Problem Is an Identity Problem: Lessons from Four Billion Years of Major Evolutionary Transitions”. Drawing on identity integration in evolutionary transitions, the work proposed a theory of alignment based on identity and set out testable predictions that distinguish it from reward-based approaches.

Carmen Ng, of the Technical University of Munich in Germany, presented “Auditing LLM-Governed Social Robots with Culture-Specific Moral Gradients”. The study assessed models’ decisions about whom a social robot should help first against culture-specific patterns of moral preference, examining calibration biases and the limits of interventions made through prompts.

Acknowledgements

The organisers thanked DataOcean AI and Chaoxing for sponsoring the workshop, and the keynote speakers, authors, reviewers and shared-task teams for their contributions. They also acknowledged the work of the organising team, volunteers and AACL-IJCNLP conference staff.

The proceedings and shared-task datasets provide a basis for further research into how language models represent cultural differences and respond to conflicting values. The organisers look forward to continuing those discussions at a future PlurVA-LLM workshop.
